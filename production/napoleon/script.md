### Hook (0:00–1:10)
W: Welcome back to HistoryVID, where today we break down the mental stamina of the man who ran an empire on four hours of sleep: Napoleon Bonaparte.
1: Picture a map table at two in the morning. Candles burning low, couriers waiting, and one short man in a grey coat still making decisions.
2: Napoleon did not conquer Europe with gunpowder and steel alone. He conquered it with speed, stamina, and raw calculation that outpaced every rival on the continent.
3: He thought faster than his enemies, slept less than his generals, and moved his armies quicker than anyone believed physically possible.
4: While enemy kings held councils for weeks, Napoleon issued complete campaign orders in hours, then rode out to check the roads himself before dawn.
5: His opponents commanded armies. Napoleon commanded time itself, squeezing three days of work out of every twenty-four hours for twenty years straight.
6: How does a human brain stay razor sharp under that pressure? That question is the real story of Napoleon Bonaparte.

### Part 1 — The 4-hour sleep & micro-naps (1:10–3:10)
7: Start with the nights. On campaign, Napoleon routinely slept three to four hours, broken into pieces, often fully dressed with maps still spread across the bed.
8: His secret weapon was the micro-nap. At any moment, even with artillery thundering nearby, he could close his eyes and sleep for fifteen minutes.
9: He woke from those short naps completely refreshed, as if he had flipped a switch in his skull that no one else could find.
10: Aides describe him napping on the ground at Austerlitz and Wagram, then rising mid-battle to dictate fresh orders without missing a single detail.
11: The foundation was mental compartmentalization. Napoleon said his mind was like a cabinet of drawers: open one subject, work it, then shut it completely.
12: When he wanted to sleep, he simply closed every drawer. Worry, he believed, was a habit, and habits could be trained away like weakness.
13: Awake, that same cabinet became a weapon. He tracked dozens of regiments in his head, their positions, supplies and marching speed, without any notes.
14: He knew where a single battalion would be standing in three days, on a road four hundred kilometers away, and was rarely wrong.
15: In his office he dictated to four secretaries at once, four different letters on four different subjects, rotating between them without losing the thread.
16: One letter on artillery, one on taxes, one on diplomacy, one on roads. Four parallel conversations running through a single, ruthlessly organized mind.
17: Doctors today would call it extraordinary working memory under sleep deprivation. His staff simply called it terrifying, and struggled to keep up with him.
18: The lesson is not to sleep less. It is to build walls between your worries, so rest is actually rest when you take it.

### Part 2 — The Grande Armée: marching as a system (3:10–5:10)
19: Now the legs. Napoleon put it bluntly: the Emperor wins his battles not with our arms, but with our legs.
20: French soldiers marched at a pace the rest of Europe considered reckless, covering ground nearly twice as fast as the standard military norm.
21: The engine behind it was the Corps system. Napoleon split his army into self-contained corps, each with its own infantry, cavalry, guns, and supplies.
22: Each corps moved on its own road, living off the land, light and fast, yet always within a day's march of its brothers.
23: In 1805, that machine produced one of the greatest marches in history: from the camps at Boulogne to the Danube, over seven hundred kilometers.
24: Two hundred thousand men crossed Europe in about five weeks, arriving behind the Austrian army at Ulm before it even knew they had left the coast.
25: General Mack surrendered at Ulm with sixty thousand men, beaten almost without a major battle. The campaign was won by boots, not bullets.
26: Napoleon planned those marches like an athlete's training program: daily distances, rest days, river crossings, and food depots calculated down to the wagon.
27: He rode the columns himself, checking boots, bread, and morale, because he knew stamina collapses first in the smallest details.
28: Speed only works when the logistics behind it hold. A fast army with no bread becomes a slow mob within three days.
29: That is the insight: endurance is a system, not a feeling. Fitness without planning burns out exactly when you need it most.
30: Train the engine, but build the supply line too: sleep, food, and recovery scheduled as seriously as the work itself. That was Napoleon's real edge.

### Part 3 — Austerlitz: patience under maximum pressure (5:10–7:10)
31: December 1805. Weeks after Ulm, Napoleon faced the combined armies of Russia and Austria near a small Moravian town called Austerlitz.
32: Two emperors stood against him, with ninety thousand men. Napoleon was outnumbered, deep in enemy country, at the end of a long supply line.
33: So he set a trap built entirely out of psychology. He deliberately weakened his own right flank, making it look fragile, almost broken.
34: He abandoned the high ground of the Pratzen Heights, retreating in plain sight, acting like a commander who had lost his nerve.
35: The allies took the bait. They swung their main force south to crush that weak right flank and cut him off from Vienna.
36: As they marched, their center stretched thin across the Pratzen Heights, exactly where Napoleon wanted the battle to be decided.
37: His generals grew nervous in the morning fog, begging him to strike early. Napoleon sat on his horse, ate breakfast, and waited.
38: He was waiting for a physical moment: the winter sun burning through the fog, and the last allied columns committing themselves to the south.
39: Then he said quietly: one sharp blow, and the war is over. Marshal Soult's corps stormed the Pratzen Heights through the clearing mist.
40: The allied center was split in two. By afternoon, the Russian and Austrian armies were shattered, fleeing across frozen ponds under French guns.
41: Austerlitz became known as his masterpiece: three emperors on one field, and the youngest, calmest mind had beaten them all.
42: Emotional control under maximum pressure is not reacting fast. It is holding still, on purpose, until the exact right moment to strike.

### Part 4 — The limits: Russia 1812 & Waterloo 1815 (7:10–8:50)
43: But stamina has laws, and in 1812 Napoleon broke them. He marched more than half a million men into Russia, chasing a battle that kept retreating.
44: The Russians traded space for time, burning crops and villages, stretching his supply lines across a thousand kilometers of hostile, empty country.
45: He reached Moscow and found it in flames. Then winter arrived, and nature presented a bill no willpower could pay.
46: On the retreat, temperatures fell below minus twenty-five. Horses died by the thousands, and exhausted men froze where they sat down to rest.
47: Of the vast army that crossed into Russia, only a broken fraction staggered back. The emperor of speed had ignored the limits of flesh.
48: By Waterloo in 1815, Napoleon's own body was failing him: stomach pain, hemorrhoids, crushing fatigue after two decades without real rest.
49: Witnesses describe him dull and drowsy during the battle's critical hours, delegating attacks he once would have directed from the front himself.
50: The sharpest military mind in Europe was still there, but the machine carrying it had finally run out of fuel.
51: Mental willpower can feel infinite. Biology is not. Recovery is not a luxury; it is the price of every performance that came before.
52: Even the greatest engine in history seized up when it stopped respecting its own maintenance. Remember that when you brag about not sleeping.

### Conclusion & takeaways (8:50–10:00)

57: Third: respect the recovery line. Willpower feels endless, but your body runs on fuel and repair, and it always collects its debts.
